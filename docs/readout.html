Where the Google Merchandise Store loses its shoppers

Ankit Singathia · GA4 data, 1 November 2020 to 31 January 2021

I looked at three months of events from Google's online merch store, using the public sample of its Google Analytics 4 export. That's 4,295,584 events from 270,154 people over 360,129 visits, ending in 5,298 orders worth $339,343. About 1.5% of visits ended in an order, and the average order was $64.

The short version

Most people who look at products never start checkout. Only 14.0% of visits that look at any products go on to checkout, and that's the biggest drop in the whole purchase path. Once someone does start checkout, most of them finish: 61% get to the payment step and 71% of those buy.

It isn't a mobile problem. Phones and desktops are within 1.2 points of each other at every step, and none of the differences is statistically significant.

The drop is much worse in five parts of the store, led by Accessories, Shop by Brand and Bags. If the sections that do worst matched a typical one, that would be roughly 685 more orders over the three months, about $41,700. That's where I'd start.

Separately, Super G Unisex Joggers looks broken: its visitors reach checkout 58.4% of the time, but only 3.6% of those checkouts end in an order.

The catch is traffic. With the number of visitors this site gets, a 21-day test could only reliably pick up a lift of about 21% or more. Proving a 10% improvement would need 144,273 people in each group, roughly 94 days. So whatever gets tested should be a real change to the page, not a small tweak.

Before trusting any of it

Google warns that this sample isn't fully consistent, so I checked the data before building anything on it. Most of it held up, but three things were broken:

  1. Add-to-cart wasn't tracked at first. It barely appears until the week of 16 November, and even after that it shows up in at most 82% of checkout visits. You can't check out without a cart, so this is missing tracking rather than missing shoppers, and I left it out of the funnel.
  2. The shipping-details event fires at the same moment as checkout. In 6,862 of 11,104 visits it's even logged a fraction of a second earlier. Put in strict order, the funnel was dropping real buyers for no reason, so I took this step out as well.
  3. Revenue stops being recorded properly at the very end. From 26 January, most orders come through at $0. Order counts are still fine, but I don't use revenue after 25 January.

I also removed 394 purchase records that had been sent twice. 906 purchase records have no order ID, so I matched duplicates on the visit and the amount instead.

Holiday revenue

The busiest week was the one starting 7 December, not Black Friday week. Revenue that week was 2.5 times the level of early November. Roughly half of that came from more visitors and half from more of them buying. Order size hardly changed.

The quietest week started 4 January, at 40% of the early-November level, mostly because of fewer visitors buying.

How I split it: revenue = visitors × visits per visitor × share of visits that buy × order size. That's an exact identity, so on a log scale the four changes add up to the total. The baseline is the average of the full weeks before Black Friday week (2 November, 9 November and 16 November). The last week of January is left out because of the revenue problem above.

The purchase path

The funnel goes from looking at products to checkout, to the payment step, to a purchase. A visit only counts at a step if it got there after the step before; the looser count next to it accepts the events in any order. Add-to-cart and shipping details are left out, for the reasons above.

Phones versus desktops

I went in expecting mobile to do worse. It doesn't: at every step phones and desktops are within 1.2 points of each other, and none of the differences is significant.

I sized each step by comparing phones with desktops rather than asking what a 10% improvement would be worth. When every step multiplies the one before, a 10% gain anywhere gives exactly 10% more orders, so that question can't tell you where to look.

Where in the store people drop off

I grouped visits by the part of the store where they first looked at products, so each visit counts once. A typical section sends 14.1% of its visits on to checkout. Five sections are well below that: Accessories (2.7%), Bags (3.8%), Office (3.8%), Drinkware (6.6%) and Shop by Brand (8.6%). If they matched the typical section, that would be roughly 685 more orders over the three months, about $41,700. That's a ceiling, because people often browse small add-ons without meaning to buy, but it's a specific place to start. I'd look at what's different about those pages: stock, prices, and whether adding to cart works the same way there.

Super G Unisex Joggers looks broken rather than weak. Its visitors reach checkout 58.4% of the time, but only 3.6% of those checkouts end in an order, against 43.4% across the site. I'd check that checkout before anything else.

Most new visitors never come back

Of people on their first ever visit, 3.8% came back the following week and 0.9% four weeks later. That's low, though maybe not surprising for a merch store that a lot of people visit once, for a gift or a conference shirt. It does mean most sales have to happen on the first visit.

I only counted someone as new if their first visit in the data was their first visit ever, and I left out weeks the data doesn't fully cover instead of counting them as zero.

Where buyers come from

Referral traffic converts best, with 2.2% of visits ending in an order. Paid Search is the weakest at 0.4%, even below people who typed the address in directly (0.6%). Email looks even better (11.7%), but that's from only 197 visits, so I wouldn't lean on it.

“Obfuscated” is traffic Google hid in this sample, and “Unknown” is a return visit that didn't carry its own source. I kept both in view rather than guessing where they came from.

If I were to test a fix

There's no A/B test in this data, so I haven't invented one. What I can do is work out what a real test would need, using the store's own numbers.

I'd measure the share of visits that end in an order, and split by person so the same person always sees the same version. In the 21 days after the holidays there were 68,260 users, 0.91% of visits converted, and people averaged 1.2 visits each.

With that traffic, the smallest lift a test could reliably catch is about 34% after a week, 25% after two weeks and 21% after three weeks.

Two-sided test at 5% significance with 80% power. A design effect above 1 means treating visits as independent would leave the test too small.

Checking the statistics on fake tests

To check the maths, I split real users into two random groups 1,000 times, with nothing different between the groups. A correct test should call about 5% of those splits significant just by chance. The delta method, which treats each person rather than each visit as the unit, flagged 5.0%. A simpler test that treats every visit as independent flagged 5.6%. Here the simpler test gets away with it because people average only 1.2 visits each. It breaks down when people visit more often, which is why I'd still use the delta method.

Using past behaviour (CUPED)

CUPED cuts noise by adjusting for what each person did before the test. Only 4.7% of these users had visited before, so it would reduce the noise by about 0.9%. That isn't worth the extra complexity here, though it would be for a test aimed at returning customers.

One more thing I'd check before reading any real result: that the two groups came out the size they were meant to be (a chi-square test on the split; stop and investigate if p is below 0.001).
