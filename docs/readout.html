The biggest leak is “View item” to “Begin checkout”, and it is the same on every device. Only 14.0% of sessions that reach “View item” go on to “Begin checkout”, the lowest rate of any step in the purchase path. Mobile and desktop differ by at most 1.2 points at any step and no gap is statistically significant, so this data does not support a device-specific fix.

A 21-day test on all users can detect a lift of 21% or more in session conversion; a 10% lift would need 144,273 users per arm, about 94 days. So test a change big enough to matter, not a button colour.

1 · What moves revenue week to week

Revenue = users × sessions per user × conversion × order value. That identity is exact, so the change in log revenue splits into four parts that add up. The baseline is the geometric mean of the full pre-holiday weeks (2020-11-02, 2020-11-09, 2020-11-16).

Revenue stops being trustworthy near the end. 450 of 5,692 purchase rows record $0 revenue; on 6 days most purchases do (2021-01-26 to 2021-01-31). Weeks that run past the last reliable day are left out of this split; conversion, which counts purchases rather than dollars, is unaffected.

The peak week, starting 2020-12-07, ran 153% above baseline. The largest part came from users (54% of the log change).

2 · Where the funnel leaks

The ordered funnel only credits a step reached after the previous one; the open funnel credits it if it happened at all. Where they differ, events arrived out of order.

Add to cart is not in the chain. 5,961 of 11,106 checkout sessions contain an add_to_cart event (53.7%); weekly coverage ranges 0% to 82%, first above 50% in the week of 2020-11-30. Nobody checks out without a cart, so this is tracking, not shoppers. A funnel through it would show a trend the shoppers never made.

Nor is shipping info. 9,483 of 11,104 sessions log add_shipping_info within 1 second of begin_checkout; in 6,862 it is logged first. One click fires both events, in random order, so as a step it measures nothing and an ordered funnel would drop real buyers at random.

5 · Designing the test

This dataset contains no experiment, and none is simulated as if it were real. This section answers what has to be settled before launch, using the real baselines above.

Population: all users in the post-holiday window (68,260 users). Metric: session conversion (sessions with a purchase / sessions), randomised by user. Baseline 0.91%, 1.23 sessions per user.

Relative lift to detectUsers per armTime to enrol both armsNaive sessions per armDesign effect

α = 0.05 two-sided, power 0.80. Design effect above 1× means a calculation that treats sessions as independent would under-power the test.

Turned around, the question a team actually asks: a 7-day test (12,355 users per arm) can detect a lift of 34% or more; a 14-day test (23,522 users per arm) can detect a lift of 25% or more; a 21-day test (34,130 users per arm) can detect a lift of 21% or more.

Would the analysis cry wolf? An A/A test on real users

Real users were split at random 1,000 times with no treatment. A correct test calls 5% of those splits significant. The delta method did so 5.0% of the time; the naive session-level test 5.6%.

Would CUPED help here?

CUPED uses each user's pre-period behaviour to cancel noise. Only 4.7% of users in the window have any pre-period history, so it cuts variance by 0.9% for conversions and 0.1% for revenue across all users, and 0.5% among users with history. Worth it for logged-in or returning-customer tests; not for a new-visitor checkout test.

Guardrail before reading any real result: a sample-ratio check on the arm split (chi-square, stop if p < 0.001).
